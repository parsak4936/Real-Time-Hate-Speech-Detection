\chapter{Evaluation and Results}\label{evaluation-and-results}

This chapter reports what the pipeline actually does. It lays out the benchmark and the metrics, gives the headline comparison across all six configurations, then looks closer at where each one wins and loses, and finishes by testing whether the differences are real or just chance. What the numbers mean for the thesis is taken up in Chapter~\ref{discussion}; the aim here is narrower, to state the results.

\section{The benchmark}\label{the-benchmark}

Every number in this chapter rests on human judgement before it rests on any model. The benchmark is \textbf{1,748 messages}, each read and labelled by hand into one of the three classes, and every score reported below is a measurement of how closely a machine reproduces those human decisions. The ground truth is therefore not a downloaded label set but the outcome of reading 1,748 live chat messages one at a time, in the context each arrived in.

The messages come from live traffic on two platforms, 1{,}259 from YouTube and 489 from Twitch, spanning eleven domains from gaming and entertainment through to relationships and news.

They are also, by construction, the hard cases, because the sampler weights its selection towards them for the reasons given in Section~\ref{datasets}. The weighting shows in the confidence distribution: Tier-1 confidence runs from 0.34 to 0.97 with a median of 0.64, and 1,536 of the 1,748 messages, 87.9 per cent, fall below the 0.80 escalation gate. The remaining 212 sit above the gate, and every one of them is a message Tier-1 called normal with high confidence, a median of 0.94. Two hundred and four of those are the random top-up; the other eight were pulled in because the two tiers had disagreed about them in spite of that confidence. So the benchmark is drawn from the second tier's own workload, with a control group of easy cases the judge should leave alone.

That 87.9 per cent describes how the benchmark was built, and it should not be mistaken for how often the pipeline escalates in ordinary running. The comparison worth making is against the collection run the benchmark was drawn from, since that holds the sampling effect apart from anything that changed between one run and another. That run captured 144,480 messages, and 8.3 per cent of them fell below the same 0.80 gate. Live chat is mostly confidently ordinary, so in normal operation the judge is called on roughly one message in twelve, against nearly nine in ten inside the benchmark. Earlier runs put the live figure between eight and fourteen per cent, so the exact rate moves with the streams being watched, but it stays an order of magnitude below the benchmark's in every case.

Of the 1,748, only \textbf{80 are toxic}, 59 offensive and 21 hateful. The other \textbf{1,668 are normal}. That is one toxic message in about twenty-two.

Because the set leans towards the hard cases, every score in this chapter describes the pipeline on that difficult slice, not on an average hour of chat. Even after weighting, eighty against 1,668 is a heavy imbalance, and that rules out the obvious metric, as the next section explains.

Every configuration is scored against the same 1,748 labels, so the comparison is fair.

\section{Metrics}\label{metrics}

On a set this lopsided, accuracy is the tempting metric and the wrong one. As Section~\ref{evaluation-methodology} shows, labelling everything ``normal'' would score over 95 per cent and catch no toxic message at all. Accuracy here rewards silence.

So the evaluation leads with the \textbf{toxic class} instead: precision, meaning of the messages flagged how many were truly toxic; recall, meaning of the truly toxic messages how many were caught; and F1, which combines the two. Three-class and binary accuracy still appear, but as background rather than headline.

Each variant's verdicts are mapped to a common label before scoring, and differences between variants are tested with McNemar's test \citep{mcnemar1947}, both as set out in Section~\ref{evaluation-methodology}.

\section{Reading the results}\label{reading-the-results}

\textbf{Tier-1 alone is a poor judge, and that is the point.} Its toxic F1 is 0.13 and its precision 0.07. In other words, when the static model says ``toxic'', it is wrong roughly fourteen times out of fifteen. That is not a failure of the design. It is why the model sits first and not last: fast and blind to context, good for surfacing suspects but not for the final word. Those false alarms are exactly what the second tier is there to clean up.

\textbf{The two-tier design keeps its main promise.} Adding the LLM judge lifts toxic F1 from 0.13 to 0.37 and precision from 0.07 to 0.27. Behind the numbers, the judge is cleaning up the static model's over-flagging. Of the 444 normal messages Tier-1 marked as toxic, the judge returned 398 to normal, and 193 of those came from gaming streams, where aggressive banter is ordinary. This answers RQ2, and it is the biggest effect in the study.

\textbf{Memory and RAG barely move the needle.} Memory nudges F1 down slightly, 0.37 to 0.34, and RAG down a touch to 0.35. Both stay next to the baseline. They did change verdicts, but the changes largely cancelled out (Section~\ref{statistical-significance}).

Section~\ref{why-memory-and-rag-stayed-neutral} goes into why this is happening, but the short answer is about the data, and it can be quantified. The benchmark comes from a single day's collection run, so few authors had time to build up a record in the database, and moderators remove the most toxic messages before a pipeline can store them (Section~\ref{limitations}), which leaves even less. Of the 1,364 distinct authors behind the 1,748 messages, 1,134, or 83.1 per cent, appear exactly once, and the median author contributes a single message. What reached the judge was thinner still: thread context arrived for almost every message, 1,739 of 1,748, but an author's earlier messages for only 150, fewer than one in ten. A memory module that sees an author's history this rarely has little to compare against, and the same thinness limits the pool of past cases RAG can draw a close precedent from.

That points to a limit of the data rather than of the modules. The benchmark holds only eighty toxic messages, for reasons Section~\ref{limitations} sets out, so the cases these modules are built for are rare, and a score driven by eighty positives leaves little room for a small effect to show.

\textbf{The likelier gap is scarcity, not the modules themselves.} Splitting the set by collection order gives a partial check on that claim. Table~\ref{tab:scaling-toxic} scores the same variants first on the early subset, which held forty-four toxic messages, and then on the full eighty. Every configuration scores higher once the extra toxic examples arrive. The ranking does move between the two sizes. With forty-four toxic messages, memory (0.33) and the multi-agent chain (0.35) both sit above the baseline (0.29); with eighty, only the multi-agent chain still does. An early lead that disappears as data arrives is what noise looks like, so the headline conclusion is unchanged, and the one configuration ahead at both sizes is also the only one whose gain in overall correctness passes the significance test in Section~\ref{statistical-significance}. But the direction is telling: as the toxic class grows, scores go up, not down. That is what a starved metric looks like, not a broken module, and it is the concrete reason Section~\ref{future-work} calls for growing the toxic class.

\textbf{The LLM-Wiki trades precision for recall, and loses the trade.} It matches the baseline on recall at 0.57, tied for the highest in the table, then drops to the lowest precision of any Tier-2 variant, 0.23, for an F1 of 0.33. The curated policy and glossary make the judge more suspicious. It catches as much toxicity as anything else and flags more innocent messages doing it, behaving rather like a moderator who has just read the rulebook cover to cover and now sees a violation everywhere.

\textbf{The multi-agent variant wins, and it wins by being careful rather than eager.} Its F1 of 0.39 is the best in the table. The shape of that win matters, though (Figure~\ref{fig:toxicprf}). It comes from the highest precision, 0.33, not the highest recall; its recall of 0.47 is in fact the lowest of the five Tier-2 variants. Splitting the decision across the Risk Scorer, Behaviour Profiler, Escalator, and Supervisor produces a more cautious judge. It flags less. When it does flag, it is right more often. Set against the baseline, the trade is concrete: it catches 38 of the eighty toxic messages instead of 46, and raises 76 false alarms instead of 123.

Two things follow from that table.

First, the escalation path is live rather than ornamental. The Escalator sent 44 messages to a person, flagged 57 on its own, and cleared the rest, which shows the three available actions are genuinely told apart instead of collapsing onto one default. The seam the design reserves for a human is therefore reached in practice, not merely declared. What it does not settle is the staffing question, and for that the benchmark figure is a poor guide. One in forty is the rate inside the benchmark, and the benchmark is not ordinary traffic. Chaining the measured rates together gives a rough sense of the live equivalent: about eight per cent of messages reach the second tier at all, and about 2.6 per cent of those are routed onward, which puts human review at roughly one message in 470. Even that is an estimate rather than a measurement, and a generous one, since the benchmark's low-confidence portion is itself weighted towards difficult cases and will overstate how often the Escalator reaches for a person. Whether any such load is workable then depends on arrival rate, on how long a reviewer needs per case, and on how many reviewers there are, none of which this study measures. The number shows the mechanism functions. Sizing it for a real moderation team is a separate exercise.

Second, the Behaviour Profiler returned low risk on \emph{every one} of the 1,748 records, covering 1,364 distinct authors. Not one came back medium or high. This is not a parse failure, since the chain falls back to ``medium'' when a response cannot be read. The profiler had little to read: in the memory run, author history came back for fewer than one record in ten (Section~\ref{reading-the-results}), so there was seldom any behaviour to profile, and the agent meant to contribute author history contributed a constant instead.

That sharpens how the multi-agent win should be read. The gain in precision cannot be coming from behavioural profiling, because that signal never varied at all. It comes from what is left: a risk score computed on content alone, a routing decision, and a supervisor forced to reconcile them. The same thin author history that flattened memory and RAG also silenced one of the four agents, and the chain still won. That suggests the benefit lies in splitting the judgement itself, rather than in any extra information the individual steps were supposed to supply.

Two results are significant, and they point in opposite directions.

The multi-agent variant fixes 79 messages the baseline got wrong and breaks only 40, a significant gain rather than luck. Most of those fixes, 75 of the 79, are normal messages it no longer flags, while 12 of the 40 it breaks are toxic messages the baseline had caught, so the significance lies in overall correctness and comes from fewer false alarms. On toxic F1 alone the gain is small: a paired bootstrap over 10,000 resamples \citep{efron1993} puts it at +0.02, with a 95 per cent interval from $-$0.05 to +0.09 that includes zero. Memory and RAG land in the middle. Each changes more than a hundred verdicts but fixes about as many of the baseline's errors as it introduces, 56 against 63 for memory and 59 against 52 for RAG, so neither difference is significant: on this set they neither helped nor hurt measurably.

\section{Throughput, scalability, and resource use}\label{throughput-scalability}

Before the numbers, a word on how they were obtained, since a performance claim is only as good as its protocol. The latency figures in Table~\ref{tab:latency} are not estimates: the pipeline records the wall-clock time of every Tier-1 inference and every baseline Tier-2 judgement as a field on the stored record, so the percentiles are computed over all 1,748 benchmark messages rather than sampled. Throughput was measured in two complementary ways, and keeping them apart matters for reading the numbers that follow. The first isolates the classifier: the same fine-tuned model runs over messages drawn from the collected stream log in a loop, with a warm-up pass excluded, model loading timed apart from inference, and Kafka and Elasticsearch out of the path, so the figure reflects the model rather than the plumbing. The second measures the pipeline as it is deployed. A replay producer publishes a fixed workload of 10,000 collected messages, drawn once with a fixed seed and replayed identically in every run, into a dedicated Kafka topic; one or more Tier-1 processors consume it, classify each message and store it in Elasticsearch exactly as they do on the live path; and every message carries its own record of when it was sent, when it was received, how long inference took, how long the write took, and when it was finished. Each configuration was run three times, the first tenth of each run was discarded as warm-up, and every run was checked for lost or duplicated messages before its numbers were used. Resource use was sampled from the operating system and the container runtime during a two-minute sustained run with the full container stack up, 27 samples in all; the script asks for one every two seconds, but each query to the container runtime takes longer than that. All three are driven by scripts kept in the repository, and the raw output of every run reported here is stored alongside them under \texttt{reports/}, so a reader can check these figures or reproduce them on their own hardware.

Latency is how long one message takes. Throughput is how many the pipeline handles per second, and it is the figure that decides whether the system can keep up with a live stream. Taking the classifier on its own first, a single Tier-1 process using every core, as the live processor does, handles \textbf{35.8 messages a second} on the laptop used throughout, with full specifications in Appendix~\ref{hardware-and-software-environment}. With the whole container stack running on the same machine it handles 27.2, because the containers compete for the same processor and memory. That is roughly 1,600 to 2,100 messages a minute, all on CPU. The traffic the pipeline actually collected sits well inside that: over the 8.4-hour June collection run, all the watched streams together averaged just under five messages a second, so one machine has room to spare.

The raw rate matters, but so does whether it grows when compute is added. Table~\ref{tab:scaling} shows what happens as classifier processes are added, each restricted to a single thread. One process manages 20.9 messages a second, two manage 33.0, and four manage 44.0, just over twice the rate of one. The gain shrinks with each step, because four processes share the laptop's four physical cores and each one slows from 20.9 to about 11 messages a second. Even so, four single-threaded processes outpace one process allowed to use every core, 44.0 against 35.8, so on this machine splitting the work across separate classifiers is the better use of the same hardware.

How far that extends needs care. The deployed Kafka topic has a single partition, so in the live pipeline only one Tier-1 consumer reads the stream at a time (Section~\ref{layer-2-the-streaming-backbone}). The figures in Table~\ref{tab:scaling} come from running the classifier itself in parallel, which measures the compute tier; Table~\ref{tab:pipeline-scaling} measures the same scaling with the whole path in place. Running more consumers against the live stream means raising the partition count first, a step this deployment did not need and so did not measure.

At half load a message is read, classified and stored in 92 milliseconds at the median, and nineteen messages in twenty are finished within 191. At eighty per cent load the median rises to 145 milliseconds and the queue starts to show: the time a message waits in Kafka grows from 4 to 51 milliseconds at the median, while inference and the write stay where they were, at about 44 and 47 milliseconds. That is what a queueing system approaching capacity looks like, and it marks the honest limit of the real-time claim. The pipeline is real-time for a stream of a few tens of messages a second, and the first thing to degrade is waiting, not processing.

The same instrumentation settles a hardware question that the thesis would otherwise have to leave open. Tier-1 runs on the processor by default, but the device is a configuration value rather than a property of the code, so the identical workload was replayed with the model on the laptop's GPU. Inference fell from 31.6 to 22.0 milliseconds at the median, yet end-to-end throughput rose only from 11.7 to 13.9 messages a second, nineteen per cent, because the Elasticsearch write was untouched and now costs more than twice the model. A spot check on identical inputs returned class probabilities agreeing to within $10^{-7}$, so the device changes speed and not verdicts. For anyone planning to spend money on this pipeline that is a usable finding: a faster classifier is the wrong purchase until the storage path is faster.

One reliability figure belongs beside these. Across roughly one hundred thousand replayed messages, in every configuration reported here, no message was lost and none was processed twice, and the work divided evenly: the busiest processor handled at most 1.08 times the load of the quietest.

Resource use under a steady load shows where the cost sits. Table~\ref{tab:resource} has the figures, taken while the classifier ran flat out with the full container stack on the same laptop. The processor carries the load, averaging 75 per cent and peaking at 89, while the GPU stays close to idle because Tier-1 runs on the CPU, as it does in the live processor. Memory, not processing, is the real limit on this machine: it sat at about 98 per cent throughout. The containers account for much of that. Together they hold about 3 GB, half of it Elasticsearch, and although their processor use is usually small, about a fifth of one core at the median, Kafka bursts to nearly two cores on its own, and with Elasticsearch the containers together passed two cores at times. That sharing has a measurable price, since the classifier's throughput fell from 35.8 to 27.2 messages a second once the stack was running.

Put next to the latency figures, these answer RQ1 directly. On one modest laptop the pipeline takes a message from the queue, labels it and stores it in 92 milliseconds at the median under half load, and sustains 35.9 messages a second once four classifier processes share the work, against the 4.8 a second the watched streams produced. For the target setting, that is real-time with room to spare.

One distinction matters here more than anything else in the section, because only half of it has been tested. That throughput grows as classifier processes are added on one machine is a measured result, and Tables~\ref{tab:scaling} and~\ref{tab:pipeline-scaling} are the evidence for it, the first for the classifier alone and the second for the pipeline end to end. That the same would hold across separate machines is not. It follows from the architecture, since a partitioned log feeding stateless consumers is built to spread precisely that way, but a design supporting something and a thesis demonstrating it are different claims. Multi-node scaling is therefore recorded here as a capability the architecture provides and a validation still outstanding, not as a finding. Running that cluster benchmark is the obvious next step, and it belongs to future work rather than to this evaluation.
