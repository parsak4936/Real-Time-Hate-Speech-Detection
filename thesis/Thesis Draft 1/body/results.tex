\chapter{Evaluation and Results}\label{evaluation-and-results}

This chapter reports what the pipeline actually does. It lays out the benchmark and the metrics, gives the headline comparison across all six configurations, then looks closer at where each one wins and loses, and finishes by testing whether the differences are real or just chance. What the numbers mean for the thesis is taken up in Chapter~\ref{discussion}; the aim here is narrower, to state the results.

\section{The benchmark}\label{the-benchmark}

Every number in this chapter rests on human judgement before it rests on any model. The benchmark is \textbf{1,748 messages}, each read and labelled by hand into one of the three classes, and every score reported below is a measurement of how closely a machine reproduces those human decisions. The ground truth is therefore not a downloaded label set but the outcome of reading 1,748 live chat messages one at a time, in the context each arrived in.

The messages come from live traffic on two platforms, 1{,}259 from YouTube and 489 from Twitch, spanning eleven domains from gaming and entertainment through to relationships and news.

They are also, by construction, the hard cases, because the sampler weights its selection towards them for the reasons given in Section~\ref{datasets}. The weighting shows in the confidence distribution: Tier-1 confidence runs from 0.34 to 0.97 with a median of 0.64, and 1,536 of the 1,748 messages, 87.9 per cent, fall below the 0.80 escalation gate. The remaining 212 sit above the gate, and every one of them is a message Tier-1 called normal with high confidence, a median of 0.94. Two hundred and four of those are the random top-up; the other eight were pulled in because the two tiers had disagreed about them in spite of that confidence. So the benchmark is drawn from the second tier's own workload, with a control group of easy cases the judge should leave alone.

That 87.9 per cent describes how the benchmark was built, and it should not be mistaken for how often the pipeline escalates in ordinary running. The comparison worth making is against the collection run the benchmark was drawn from, since that holds the sampling effect apart from anything that changed between one run and another. That run captured 144,480 messages, and 8.3 per cent of them fell below the same 0.80 gate. Live chat is mostly confidently ordinary, so in normal operation the judge is called on roughly one message in twelve, against nearly nine in ten inside the benchmark. Earlier runs put the live figure between eight and fourteen per cent, so the exact rate moves with the streams being watched, but it stays an order of magnitude below the benchmark's in every case.

Of the 1,748, only \textbf{80 are toxic}, 59 offensive and 21 hateful. The other \textbf{1,668 are normal}. That is one toxic message in about twenty-two.

Because the set leans towards the hard cases, every score in this chapter describes the pipeline on that difficult slice, not on an average hour of chat. Even after weighting, eighty against 1,668 is a heavy imbalance, and that rules out the obvious metric, as the next section explains.

Every configuration is scored against the same 1,748 labels, so the comparison is fair.

\section{Metrics}\label{metrics}

On a set this lopsided, accuracy is the tempting metric and the wrong one. As Section~\ref{evaluation-methodology} shows, labelling everything ``normal'' would score over 95 per cent and catch no toxic message at all. Accuracy here rewards silence.

So the evaluation leads with the \textbf{toxic class} instead: precision, meaning of the messages flagged how many were truly toxic; recall, meaning of the truly toxic messages how many were caught; and F1, which combines the two. Three-class and binary accuracy still appear, but as background rather than headline.

Each variant's verdicts are mapped to a common label before scoring, and differences between variants are tested with McNemar's test \citep{mcnemar1947}, both as set out in Section~\ref{evaluation-methodology}.

\section{Reading the results}\label{reading-the-results}

\textbf{Tier-1 alone is a poor judge, and that is the point.} Its toxic F1 is 0.13 and its precision 0.07. In other words, when the static model says ``toxic'', it is wrong roughly fourteen times out of fifteen. That is not a failure of the design. It is why the model sits first and not last: fast and blind to context, good for surfacing suspects but not for the final word. Those false alarms are exactly what the second tier is there to clean up.

\textbf{The two-tier design keeps its main promise.} Adding the LLM judge lifts toxic F1 from 0.13 to 0.37 and precision from 0.07 to 0.27. Behind the numbers, the judge is cleaning up the static model's over-flagging. Of the 444 normal messages Tier-1 marked as toxic, the judge returned 398 to normal, and 193 of those came from gaming streams, where aggressive banter is ordinary. This answers RQ2, and it is the biggest effect in the study.

\textbf{Memory and RAG barely move the needle.} Memory nudges F1 down slightly, 0.37 to 0.34, and RAG down a touch to 0.35. Both stay next to the baseline. They did change verdicts, but the changes largely cancelled out (Section~\ref{statistical-significance}).

Section~\ref{why-memory-and-rag-stayed-neutral} goes into why this is happening, but the short answer is about the data, and it can be quantified. The benchmark comes from a single day's collection run, so few authors had time to build up a record in the database, and moderators remove the most toxic messages before a pipeline can store them (Section~\ref{limitations}), which leaves even less. Of the 1,364 distinct authors behind the 1,748 messages, 1,134, or 83.1 per cent, appear exactly once, and the median author contributes a single message. What reached the judge was thinner still: thread context arrived for almost every message, 1,739 of 1,748, but an author's earlier messages for only 150, fewer than one in ten. A memory module that sees an author's history this rarely has little to compare against, and the same thinness limits the pool of past cases RAG can draw a close precedent from.

That points to a limit of the data rather than of the modules. The benchmark holds only eighty toxic messages, for reasons Section~\ref{limitations} sets out, so the cases these modules are built for are rare, and a score driven by eighty positives leaves little room for a small effect to show.

\textbf{The likelier gap is scarcity, not the modules themselves.} Splitting the set by collection order gives a partial check on that claim. Table~\ref{tab:scaling-toxic} scores the same variants first on the early subset, which held forty-four toxic messages, and then on the full eighty. Every configuration scores higher once the extra toxic examples arrive. The ranking does move between the two sizes. With forty-four toxic messages, memory (0.33) and the multi-agent chain (0.35) both sit above the baseline (0.29); with eighty, only the multi-agent chain still does. An early lead that disappears as data arrives is what noise looks like, so the headline conclusion is unchanged, and the one configuration ahead at both sizes is also the only one whose gain in overall correctness passes the significance test in Section~\ref{statistical-significance}. But the direction is telling: as the toxic class grows, scores go up, not down. That is what a starved metric looks like, not a broken module, and it is the concrete reason Section~\ref{future-work} calls for growing the toxic class.

\textbf{The LLM-Wiki trades precision for recall, and loses the trade.} It matches the baseline on recall at 0.57, tied for the highest in the table, then drops to the lowest precision of any Tier-2 variant, 0.23, for an F1 of 0.33. The curated policy and glossary make the judge more suspicious. It catches as much toxicity as anything else and flags more innocent messages doing it, behaving rather like a moderator who has just read the rulebook cover to cover and now sees a violation everywhere.

\textbf{The multi-agent variant wins, and it wins by being careful rather than eager.} Its F1 of 0.39 is the best in the table. The shape of that win matters, though (Figure~\ref{fig:toxicprf}). It comes from the highest precision, 0.33, not the highest recall; its recall of 0.47 is in fact the lowest of the five Tier-2 variants. Splitting the decision across the Risk Scorer, Behaviour Profiler, Escalator, and Supervisor produces a more cautious judge. It flags less. When it does flag, it is right more often. Set against the baseline, the trade is concrete: it catches 38 of the eighty toxic messages instead of 46, and raises 76 false alarms instead of 123.

Two things follow from that table.

First, the escalation path is live rather than ornamental. The Escalator sent 44 messages to a person, flagged 57 on its own, and cleared the rest, which shows the three available actions are genuinely told apart instead of collapsing onto one default. The seam the design reserves for a human is therefore reached in practice, not merely declared. What it does not settle is the staffing question, and for that the benchmark figure is a poor guide. One in forty is the rate inside the benchmark, and the benchmark is not ordinary traffic. Chaining the measured rates together gives a rough sense of the live equivalent: about eight per cent of messages reach the second tier at all, and about 2.6 per cent of those are routed onward, which puts human review at roughly one message in 470. Even that is an estimate rather than a measurement, and a generous one, since the benchmark's low-confidence portion is itself weighted towards difficult cases and will overstate how often the Escalator reaches for a person. Whether any such load is workable then depends on arrival rate, on how long a reviewer needs per case, and on how many reviewers there are, none of which this study measures. The number shows the mechanism functions. Sizing it for a real moderation team is a separate exercise.

Second, the Behaviour Profiler returned low risk on \emph{every one} of the 1,748 records, covering 1,364 distinct authors. Not one came back medium or high. This is not a parse failure, since the chain falls back to ``medium'' when a response cannot be read. The profiler had little to read: in the memory run, author history came back for fewer than one record in ten (Section~\ref{reading-the-results}), so there was seldom any behaviour to profile, and the agent meant to contribute author history contributed a constant instead.

That sharpens how the multi-agent win should be read. The gain in precision cannot be coming from behavioural profiling, because that signal never varied at all. It comes from what is left: a risk score computed on content alone, a routing decision, and a supervisor forced to reconcile them. The same thin author history that flattened memory and RAG also silenced one of the four agents, and the chain still won. That suggests the benefit lies in splitting the judgement itself, rather than in any extra information the individual steps were supposed to supply.

Two results are significant, and they point in opposite directions.

The multi-agent variant fixes 79 messages the baseline got wrong and breaks only 40, a significant gain rather than luck. Most of those fixes, 75 of the 79, are normal messages it no longer flags, while 12 of the 40 it breaks are toxic messages the baseline had caught, so the significance lies in overall correctness and comes from fewer false alarms. On toxic F1 alone the gain is small: a paired bootstrap over 10,000 resamples \citep{efron1993} puts it at +0.02, with a 95 per cent interval from $-$0.05 to +0.09 that includes zero. The step from Tier-1 to the Tier-2 baseline is a different matter, +0.24 with an interval from +0.17 to +0.32. The LLM-Wiki does the reverse: it changes many verdicts, 136 in all, more than any other variant, but the baseline is right on 85 of them against the Wiki's 51, so its over-flagging is a real effect rather than noise. Memory and RAG land in the middle. Each changes more than a hundred verdicts but fixes about as many of the baseline's errors as it introduces, 56 against 63 for memory and 59 against 52 for RAG, so neither difference is significant: on this set they neither helped nor hurt measurably.

\section{Throughput, scalability, and resource use}\label{throughput-scalability}

Before the numbers, a word on how they were obtained, since a performance claim is only as good as its protocol. The latency figures in Table~\ref{tab:latency} are not estimates: the pipeline records the wall-clock time of every Tier-1 inference and every baseline Tier-2 judgement as a field on the stored record, so the percentiles are computed over all 1,748 benchmark messages rather than sampled. Throughput was measured in two complementary ways, and keeping them apart matters for reading the numbers that follow. The first isolates the classifier: the same fine-tuned model runs over messages drawn from the collected stream log in a loop, with a warm-up pass excluded, model loading timed apart from inference, and Kafka and Elasticsearch out of the path, so the figure reflects the model rather than the plumbing. The second measures the pipeline as it is deployed. A replay producer publishes a fixed workload of 10,000 collected messages, drawn once with a fixed seed and replayed identically in every run, into a dedicated Kafka topic created with as many partitions as there are consumers, since a single-partition topic would leave every extra consumer idle; one or more Tier-1 processors consume it, classify each message and store it in a run-specific Elasticsearch index, never the live one, but otherwise exactly as they do on the live path; and every message carries its own record of when it was sent, when it was received, how long inference took, how long the write took, and when it was finished. Every configuration was run at least three times, the first tenth of each run was discarded as warm-up, and each run was checked for lost or duplicated messages before its numbers were used. Both devices were measured the same way, since the device Tier-1 runs on is a configuration value rather than a property of the code. Resource use was sampled from the operating system and the container runtime during a two-minute sustained run with the full container stack up, 27 samples in all; the script asks for one every two seconds, but each query to the container runtime takes longer than that. All three are driven by scripts kept in the repository, and the raw output of every run reported here is stored alongside them under \texttt{reports/}, so a reader can check these figures or reproduce them on their own hardware.

Latency is how long one message takes. Throughput is how many the pipeline handles per second, and it is the figure that decides whether the system can keep up with a live stream. Taking the classifier on its own first, a single Tier-1 process using every core, as the live processor does, handles \textbf{35.8 messages a second} on the laptop used throughout, with full specifications in Appendix~\ref{hardware-and-software-environment}. With the whole container stack running on the same machine it handles 27.2, because the containers compete for the same processor and memory. That is roughly 1,600 to 2,100 messages a minute, all on CPU. The traffic the pipeline actually collected sits well inside that: over the 8.4-hour June collection run, all the watched streams together averaged just under five messages a second, so one machine has room to spare.

The raw rate matters, but so does whether it grows when compute is added. Table~\ref{tab:scaling} shows what happens as classifier processes are added, each restricted to a single thread. One process manages 20.9 messages a second, two manage 33.0, and four manage 44.0, just over twice the rate of one. The gain shrinks with each step, because four processes share the laptop's four physical cores and each one slows from 20.9 to about 11 messages a second. Even so, four single-threaded processes outpace one process allowed to use every core, 44.0 against 35.8, so on this machine splitting the work across separate classifiers is the better use of the same hardware.

How far that extends needs care. The deployed Kafka topic has a single partition, so in the live pipeline only one Tier-1 consumer reads the stream at a time (Section~\ref{layer-2-the-streaming-backbone}). The figures in Table~\ref{tab:scaling} come from running the classifier itself in parallel, which measures the compute tier; Table~\ref{tab:pipeline-scaling} measures the same scaling with the whole path in place. Running more consumers against the live stream means raising the partition count first, a step this deployment never needed. The end-to-end runs below therefore use a separate replay topic whose partition count matches the number of consumers, which is the same one-line change an operator would make to the live topic.

Three things stand out. The first is that the headroom survives the stricter measurement: four processors on the processor sustain 35.9 messages a second, about seven times the 4.8 a second the watched streams actually produced. The shape of the scaling repeats what the classifier alone showed, near-linear at two processes with an efficiency of 0.94, falling to 0.77 at four, because four classifiers now share four physical cores with Kafka and Elasticsearch. The inference column shows that contention directly: the median time a message spends in the model grows from 32 to 38 to 56 milliseconds as processes are added, while the write to storage does not move.

The second is where the level is set. One process moves 11.7 messages a second end to end against 20.9 for the classifier on its own, and the per-message records show exactly where the difference goes: writing the labelled record to Elasticsearch takes about 47 milliseconds, as much as the model itself on the processor and nearly four times as much on the GPU, and that cost is flat across every configuration measured on this machine. The last four words matter, and the cluster measurements later in this section are what put them there: the same Elasticsearch image, the same heap setting and the same client write a record in about 5 milliseconds on the cluster's Linux hosts. The 47 milliseconds is therefore a property of this particular host rather than of Elasticsearch or of the design. The most likely explanation is that Docker Desktop on Windows routes container traffic through a virtual machine and adds a fixed cost to every request; what is certain is the measurement, not its cause, and nothing below depends on which explanation is right. A simple model follows. If each process pays inference plus write on every message, and the two do not overlap, throughput should be the number of processes divided by their sum. That predicts 12.6, 23.7 and 38.9 messages a second for the three CPU rows against 11.7, 21.9 and 35.9 measured, and 14.3, 33.6 and 68.0 for the GPU rows against 13.9, 30.4 and 64.6. The prediction is high every time, by between three and eleven per cent, and what it leaves out is the Kafka hop and the per-message bookkeeping. The pipeline behaves like what it is: one serial per-message cost, repeated across as many processes as the machine can run.

The third is the one worth stating carefully, because it looks better than it is. On the GPU the speed-up is 2.18 at two processes and 4.65 at four, efficiencies of 1.09 and 1.16, which reads as faster than linear. The device is not doing anything magical. The single-process baseline simply wastes it: with one process the card sits idle through tokenisation, the Kafka hop and the 48-millisecond write, and the resource samples confirm it, showing four processes holding 1.6 GB of the card's 4 GB and keeping it only 38 per cent busy. Adding processes overlaps one process's storage wait with another's inference, so the efficiency above one measures how poor the baseline was, not scaling that beats the number of processes.

Throughput says how much the pipeline can absorb. It says nothing about what one message experiences, and for a moderation system that is the more important question. The same workload was therefore replayed at two fixed arrival rates below capacity, 18 and 29 messages a second, half and eighty per cent of the four-processor figure on the processor, and the lower of the two was replayed again with the same four processes on the GPU. That is what a queueing system approaching capacity looks like, and it marks the honest limit of the real-time claim. The pipeline is real-time for a stream of a few tens of messages a second, and the first thing to degrade is waiting, not processing.

The same rate on the GPU cuts the median to 66 milliseconds, a quarter faster, and the inference column says why: 13 milliseconds against 33. The tail does not follow. The GPU's 99th percentile is the worse of the two, 318 milliseconds against 234, because four processes take turns on one card and a message arriving behind the others waits for them. For a moderation queue that trade is usually worth taking, but it should be stated rather than buried: the GPU improves the typical message and not the worst one.

That comparison settles a hardware question the thesis would otherwise have to leave open, and it has two readings of which only one is right. Measured at a single process, the GPU looks like a poor investment: inference falls from 32 to 22 milliseconds at the median, yet end-to-end throughput rises only from 11.7 to 13.9 messages a second, nineteen per cent, because the Elasticsearch write is untouched and now costs more than twice the model. Measured at four processes the judgement reverses: 64.6 messages a second against 35.9 on the processor, an eighty per cent gain, because four processes are enough to keep the card working while each of them waits on storage. The useful finding is therefore conditional rather than flat. A faster classifier is not worthless, but on this machine it is worthless on its own: the GPU only pays for itself once enough processes run to hide the write behind it. That conclusion is tied to this machine's storage path, and the cluster shows how tightly. Where the write costs 5 milliseconds instead of 47, nothing needs hiding, and the model becomes the dominant per-message cost again. The transferable lesson is not that storage is the bottleneck but that the bottleneck moves: on a laptop running Docker Desktop it sits in the write, on ordinary Linux hosts it sits back in the model, and a deployment should be measured before anything is bought to make it faster. A spot check on identical inputs returned class probabilities agreeing to within $10^{-7}$, so the device changes speed and not verdicts.

One reliability figure belongs beside these. Across the 280,000 messages replayed in the 28 single-machine runs reported above, on both devices, no message was lost and none was processed twice, and the work divided evenly: the busiest processor handled at most 1.04 times the load of the quietest.

Within a single cluster machine the curve is the familiar one: 12.7 messages a second with one process, 23.4 with two and 38.8 with four, so four cores return just over three times the work of one. Adding a second whole machine then raises throughput to 61.1, a factor of 1.58 rather than 2, and the per-machine figures explain the shortfall rather than leaving it a puzzle. The two machines are identically specified and ran identical software, yet the pure worker sustained 36.4 messages a second while the machine that also hosts Kafka and Elasticsearch managed 29.9, about eighteen per cent less. The cost of co-locating the broker and the store with a worker is therefore a measurable tax rather than a rounding error, and a deployment with a dedicated broker node would be expected to come closer to the ideal. Across every cluster run reported here no message was lost and none was processed twice.

The last row of Table~\ref{tab:cluster-scaling} is the one that repays attention, because it moves in the wrong direction. Adding the three Raspberry Pi boards to the two virtual machines does not raise throughput from 61.1; it cuts it to 29.7, less than half, with five machines and twenty processors working instead of two and eight. Nothing failed: those runs lost no messages either. The per-node timings say exactly what happened. The two virtual machines finished the share they had been given in about 113 seconds and then sat idle, while the boards needed between 207 and 225 seconds for theirs, and the run is not over until the slowest consumer finishes. Kafka assigns partitions to consumers without regard to how fast a consumer is, so a board that classifies at 4 messages a second is handed the same quantity of work as a virtual machine that classifies at 25.

That is worth stating as a result rather than an anomaly, because it is the kind of thing an architecture diagram cannot tell you. Horizontal scaling pays when the machines added are comparable to the machines already there. Add slower ones under an assignment policy that treats all consumers alike, and the fast machines spend most of the run waiting, so the cluster performs worse than its fast subset alone.

Because Kafka divides partitions equally between consumers, the share a machine receives can be weighted by giving it more consumers. The final row of Table~\ref{tab:cluster-scaling} does exactly that: twelve processes on each virtual machine against four on each board, so the two fast machines claim twenty-four of the thirty-six partitions. Throughput rises from 29.7 to 36.3 messages a second, a gain of a fifth, with nothing lost or duplicated. The principle holds, and it is the one the supervisor's suggestion of distributing work across hardware tiers depends on.

The gain is real but partial, and the per-node figures say why. The pure worker reached 34.2 messages a second under the weighted split, but the machine that also hosts Kafka and Elasticsearch managed only 11.7, because twelve classifier processes on four cores now compete with the broker and the store for the same hardware. Weighting by process count is therefore a blunt instrument: it buys a larger share of the work at the cost of over-subscribing the machine that receives it. A scheduler that assigned partitions in proportion to measured throughput, without multiplying consumers, would capture the gain without the contention. Even then the honest reading of the last three rows is that five unequal machines, however the work is divided, did not beat two matched ones: 36.3 against 61.1. Heterogeneous scale-out is worth engineering when the slow devices are what you have, not when faster ones are already available.

Two further observations are worth recording. A single cluster machine with four processors reaches 38.8 messages a second against the laptop's 35.9 with the same number of processors, so the two four-core machines are close. And the headline comparison answers the question the architecture had only asserted: the ceiling rises when machines are added, not merely when processes are added to one machine.

Those two machines reach almost the same throughput by opposite routes, and the per-stage timings show it plainly. The laptop classifies a message in 56 milliseconds and then spends 47 storing it; the cluster machine classifies in 83 and stores in 5. The model is slower on the virtual machine and the write is an order of magnitude faster, and the two differences nearly cancel. Since the Elasticsearch version, its heap setting, the client library and the index mapping are identical in both places, the difference is the host environment and not the database: on Windows the container traffic passes through Docker Desktop's virtual machine, on the cluster it does not. The practical consequence was stated above where the GPU was discussed, and it is worth repeating because it is easy to generalise wrongly from a single machine. Which stage dominates is a property of the deployment, not of the pipeline.

The three Raspberry Pi boards were prepared exactly as the virtual machines were, with the same pinned libraries and the same checksum-verified model weights, and reaching them took one piece of debugging worth recording because it is a trap any multi-node deployment of this kind can fall into. The boards could not open a connection to the broker at all, in either direction, although the virtual machines could reach the boards. The cause was not a firewall but an address collision: Docker's default bridge network on the broker's host occupies 172.17.0.0/16, which is exactly the subnet the boards sit on, so the host treated every board address as local to its own container network and the replies never left the machine. Moving the bridge to a different range resolved it.

With that fixed, each board was also measured on its own under the full protocol, and the last two rows of Table~\ref{tab:cluster-scaling} are the result. The 8 GB board sustains 6.2 messages a second across two runs that agree to within 0.02, and the 4 GB board 4.1, with nothing lost or duplicated in either case. The difference between them is memory rather than cores, since both have four, and it shows up in the model: 557 milliseconds a message on the larger board against 821 on the smaller. Both are roughly an order of magnitude slower than a virtual machine, which needs 83. Set against the 4.8 messages a second the watched streams actually produced, one 8 GB board keeps up with the observed load with about a quarter in hand, and the 4 GB board does not quite.

Dedicated runs on a single board, three repeats of the full 10,000-message workload, were also attempted. Every one of them failed to finish, on both the 4 GB and the 8 GB board, and the way they failed is more instructive than a throughput figure would have been. The boards were not idle and nothing crashed: across one such run the four processes performed 10,081 classifications, which is the whole workload, yet only 3,521 distinct messages were ever completed. Three and a half thousand messages were classified more than once, several of them five times over, and the run made no net progress through the backlog.

The cause is a default. A Kafka consumer is evicted from its group if it does not return for more messages within \texttt{max.poll.interval.ms}, five minutes as shipped, and it fetches up to five hundred records in one go. A virtual machine classifying in 83 milliseconds works through five hundred records in about forty seconds and is never close to the limit. A board classifying in roughly 800 milliseconds needs around four hundred seconds for the same batch, overruns the limit, and is declared dead by the broker. Its partition is reassigned, the records it had not yet committed are delivered again, and the cycle repeats. Nothing in the pipeline reports an error while this happens, which is what makes it worth recording: the symptom is a cluster that stays busy and never finishes.

The five-machine runs did not hit this because the work was spread more thinly. With twenty consumers over the same workload each board held roughly 360 messages, finished them inside the five-minute window, and committed normally, which is why those runs completed with nothing lost and are the ones the per-board figures above come from. The lesson generalises past this hardware. Stream-processing defaults encode an assumption about how fast a consumer works, and constrained devices violate it; deploying this pipeline to edge hardware is a matter of configuration, smaller fetch batches or a longer poll interval, before it is a matter of capacity. That is a cheaper fix than more machines, and it would not have been visible without running the workload on a slow device.

One distinction still matters, because only part of this has been tested. That throughput grows as classifier processes are added on one machine is a measured result, and Tables~\ref{tab:scaling} and~\ref{tab:pipeline-scaling} are the evidence for it, the first for the classifier alone and the second for the pipeline end to end. That it also grows when whole machines are added is now measured too, on two machines, and Table~\ref{tab:cluster-scaling} is the evidence. What remains untested is the shape of that curve beyond two: whether a third and fourth machine keep paying, and where the broker itself becomes the ceiling. Two points establish a direction, not a limit. Nor has the pipeline been measured on constrained edge hardware, for the network reason given above. Those are the honest boundaries of the scaling claim, and both are recorded as future work rather than inferred here.

Latency on the cluster was measured the same way as on the laptop, by replaying at fixed arrival rates below capacity, and Table~\ref{tab:pipeline-latency} carries both. A message crosses the two-machine deployment in 105 milliseconds at the median against the laptop's 89, slower despite the cluster's far higher throughput, and the inference column explains it: the virtual machine's processor needs 83 milliseconds for the model where the laptop needs 33. The cluster buys capacity, not per-message speed. What the cluster evaluation still lacks is host-level resource sampling: the probe records the containers' footprint on the broker node, where Elasticsearch holds 2.2 GB and Kafka 1.1 GB of the 31 GB available, but processor and memory utilisation of the hosts themselves was captured only on the laptop. That gap does not touch any result reported here.

Put next to the latency figures, these answer RQ1 directly. On one modest laptop the pipeline takes a message from the queue, labels it and stores it in 89 milliseconds at the median under half load, and sustains 35.9 messages a second once four classifier processes share the work, or 64.6 with those same four processes on the GPU. Across two machines of the university cluster it reaches 61.1. Every one of those figures sits far above the 4.8 messages a second the watched streams actually produced, and the lowest of them is more than twice it. For the target setting, that is real-time with room to spare, on a laptop and on a cluster alike.
