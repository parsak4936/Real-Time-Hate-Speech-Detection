# Core Moderation Policy

The foundational definitions and principles. This page is always consulted.

## The three labels

- **HATE** — content that attacks, dehumanises, threatens, or incites violence
  against people because of a protected characteristic (race, ethnicity, national
  origin, religion, gender, sexual orientation, disability, or serious disease).
  Slurs used as attacks, calls for exclusion or violence, and dehumanising
  comparisons all fall here.
- **OFFENSIVE** — content that is abusive, harassing, vulgar, or demeaning, but not
  tied to a protected characteristic. Personal insults, profanity aimed at someone,
  and general nastiness are offensive rather than hateful.
- **NORMAL** — everything else, including heated disagreement, criticism, dark
  humour, and domain-appropriate banter that is not aimed at a person or a group in
  a harmful way.

## Intent and impact, not vocabulary

The same word can land in different classes depending on who says it, to whom, and
why. A slur quoted to condemn it is not hate; the same slur aimed at a person is.
Decide from the intent behind the message and its likely impact on the target, not
from the mere presence of a "bad word".

## Protected characteristics

Attacks tied to these move a message from OFFENSIVE toward HATE: race, ethnicity,
national origin, immigration status, religion, gender and gender identity, sexual
orientation, disability, and serious illness. Attacks on someone's opinions,
behaviour, team, or company are not protected-characteristic attacks.

## Decision principles

- Target matters. "You're an idiot" is offensive; "[group] are subhuman" is hate.
- A threat of violence is treated at the severe end, regardless of domain.
- Criticism of a government, a policy, a company, or a public figure's actions is
  normally NORMAL, even when angry, unless it turns into a protected-characteristic
  attack or a threat.
- When genuinely ambiguous, weigh the domain (see the domain pages) and the coded-
  language and edge-case guidance before deciding.
